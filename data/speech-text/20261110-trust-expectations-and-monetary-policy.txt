Introduction
It’s an honour to give the 24th Sir Leslie Melville Lecture.
My theme today is trust. Trust is central to all of our lives. We trust our families and friends. We
trust the food we eat. We trust companies with our personal data. We trust governments to keep
us safe.

But few rely on trust as profoundly as central banks. And that’s because money itself is based entirely
on a social contract. Banknotes in our wallets or deposits in our bank accounts have no intrinsic value
beyond the monetary realm. Their value rests on trust that others will accept them tomorrow at
much the same worth. And that trust, in turn, relies on confidence that central banks will keep
prices stable. So trust isn’t just a source of legitimacy – it’s integral to how monetary policy
actually works.
That trust must be earned. And the decades following the 1980s were good times for central banks
around the globe, as flexible inflation targeting delivered consistently low and stable inflation
alongside rising real incomes. But more recently, and particularly since COVID, things have been
rockier: persistent above-target inflation has marred that hard-won track record. The broader climate
of trust towards public bodies has deteriorated. And we’ve entered a ‘VUCA’ (Volatile, Uncertain,
Complex and Ambiguous) world, amid a fusillade of shocks from tariffs, wars, geopolitical tensions,
pandemics, and technological and environmental change. That means inflation is more likely to be
pushed off target; economic forecasts are more likely to be wrong; and policy trade-offs get uglier.
Just as we need it most, trust is under strain.
These challenges are not unique to Australia. And, whatever the commentary may say, they’re far
from unprecedented. Leslie Melville definitely had it worse. He joined the Commonwealth Bank – the
RBA’s predecessor – as its first (and initially only) economist in 1931, a year in which Australian GDP
fell by nearly 7 per cent, amid the depths of the Great Depression. The following year, unemployment
reached nearly 30 per cent – an unbelievable figure to modern eyes. The financial instability,
world war, fierce economic and political conflict and institutional upheaval that followed frankly puts
today’s challenges into humbling perspective.
Coming from the United Kingdom, 10,000 miles away, I wouldn’t have expected any personal
connection to Melville. But it turns out I have two. The Bank of England, my previous employer,
recommended him for the job. More than 90 years later, as another newcomer to Australian
policymaking, I have the joy of working with the RBA’s world-class economics team: a far cry from
Melville’s empty office! And while writing this speech, I discovered that the headmaster of my old
high school in the United Kingdom in the 1980s – known to us all simply as ‘Mr Melville’ – was in fact
Leslie’s son. Tony Melville was born in Adelaide, grew up in Sydney when Leslie joined the Bank,
and travelled the opposite way to me: studying first at King’s College in Cambridge before taking up a
career in British education. Few, if any, of his pupils knew his extraordinary history.

In my speech today I want to develop the theme of trust – discussing, first, what trust means in
everyday life; second, why monetary policy needs it so badly; and third, how that trust is being
challenged today around the world. I will finish by setting out how the RBA is responding here
in Australia.
How we talk about ‘trust’ in our everyday lives
When we say we trust someone, we usually mean one of two things.
Model 1 is the trust we give to family and close friends. It’s instinctive, heart-felt. Family ties, shared
history and values mean we know they will be on our side: honest, open and straight. Not perfect, of
course. A trusted friend may get things wrong, be stubborn, even drive us up the wall! But deep
down we know they are loyal, and it takes a lot to dislodge that. This is trust rooted in character
and relationship. We don’t need a fresh demonstration of it every time we meet: we lean on what
we know.
Model 2 describes the trust we place in experts: doctors, lawyers, aircraft pilots. Honesty and
integrity still matter. But the key test is competence: do they have the qualifications, skills and tools
to do complex, sometimes life- (or livelihood-) threatening, things on our behalf? What if they’re
useless, crooked – or both?
Answering this question involves a Catch-22 – because the very thing that creates the need for an
expert in the first place (our inability to do the task) makes it impossible to audit them ourselves. And
that creates an incentive problem. At one extreme, if experts know we have blind faith in them,
there’s no incentive for competence. But, at the other extreme, if we’re perpetually sceptical, nothing
ever gets done. To address this problem, we demand transparency: show us your credentials,
your track record, your reasoning. But sometimes it takes an expert to understand an expert. So we
also need accountability – independent expert scrutiny beforehand; assessment of outcomes
afterwards. ‘Trust, but verify’ – as Ronald Reagan used to say.
For a public institution, like a central bank, all of these concepts matter (Figure 1):
We want an institution that’s on our side, like a friend – honest, consistent and reliable, serving
our long-term interests not selfish or sectional ones.
We want an institution that’s up to the job, like a trusted professional – with the right skills, tools
and judgment; and a track record of delivering strong outcomes.

We want an institution that can explain itself clearly and consistently – its objectives, its
decisions, the costs and uncertainties, and its mistakes, when they happen.
And we want an institution that’s accountable for its actions, both ahead of time, and after
the event.
These qualities reinforce one another. Competence without integrity or public purpose won’t sustain
trust. But neither will good intentions without capability. We need confidence that an institution can
explain its actions, and bear the consequences. Transparency and accountability help us judge that.
Why earning public trust is particularly important for
monetary policy
This setup applies to many public institutions. So what’s distinctive about monetary policy?
First, it works with a lag: and pain often precedes gain. William McChesney Martin vividly described
the central bank as a ‘chaperone who has ordered the punch bowl removed just when the party was
really warming up’. When excessive inflationary pressures build, monetary policy has to be

tightened, restraining demand. Over time, weaker demand helps reduce inflation, returning the
economy to balance. But while those lags are working through, and people are having to make
difficult and costly adjustments, they need assurance that we’re acting competently. Has the central
bank demonstrated that it’s acting in the public’s long-term interests? Has it been clear about its
reasons for acting – and costs of not acting? And has it held itself open to challenge and scrutiny?
That matters even more when the inflationary pressure is associated with adverse supply
developments, which brings additional and unavoidable pain. Taking away the punch bowl is rarely a
way to win instant popularity. The question is whether people can see why the difficult decision is
necessary, even when its immediate effects are unwelcome.
These things aren’t unique to monetary policy: public health measures (e.g. vaccinations),
environmental policy or macroprudential adjustment share similar features. But what is distinctive is
trust directly affects how well monetary policy works. And that’s because the impact of today’s policy
decision depends on whether people trust us to do what’s needed tomorrow too. In simple terms: do
they expect us to return the economy to balance, with on-target inflation and full employment, in the
long run? In more technical terms: do they understand and trust our ‘reaction function’ (i.e. how we
adjust interest rates as the economy evolves in order to achieve those goals)?
To illustrate how beliefs about the future can affect policy effectiveness today, consider a thought
experiment using one of the RBA’s models, in which the central bank lifts rates for a few quarters to
return inflation to target (Graph 1). The red lines describe a scenario in which people don’t
expect the tightening to be persistent – so each period, tighter policy surprises them. Spending (and
hence inflation) fall only a little, as people fail to account for future policy. By contrast, the orange
lines show a scenario in which people fully understand and believe the central bank’s strategy from
the start. They adjust spending today not just in response to today’s policy settings, but in
anticipation of future settings too. Although the realised path for interest rates is identical in both
cases, output and inflation respond sooner, and by more, when the policy response is trusted.
14[]
This is a powerful result – because it means that if the public understands and trusts the central
bank’s strategy, the policy response required to deliver a given disinflation can be smaller and less
costly in terms of lost output.
To illustrate this with a topical example, Graph 2 uses the same model to trace the effects of a
temporary increase in overseas costs, such as oil prices, under different assumptions of trust in the
policy response. Both situations show the same path for inflation. What differs is the demand
adjustment needed to bring it back to target.
The pink lines show what happens when people do not trust the central bank to return inflation to
target in a timely way. Businesses are inclined to pass on more of the cost increases, expecting
that a slow policy response will allow cost pressures to persist and demand to keep expanding. And
employees are minded to seek higher wages. The temporary cost shock risks becoming embedded in
prices and wages. To bring inflation back promptly, the central bank must raise rates quite
aggressively, contracting demand sharply.

Measuring public trust in central banks
So trust matters. But economic developments are putting it to the test.
Not all the news is bad. GDP growth and employment, explicit or implicit goals for all central banks,
have proved resilient in many countries. Despite the sharp tightening in policy since the end of
COVID, unemployment is much lower than might have been expected (Graph 4).
But inflation is another story. It’s been above central bank targets for years (Graph 5). And around
the world, that’s causing increasing anger. Consumer confidence is exceptionally weak. And
households rank inflation as their number one economic concern.

But some of this variation reflects country-specific factors. And the frequently claimed long-run

some institutions has declined over time, others (including the civil service, the police and armed

Inflation expectations – an alternative, and more longstanding trust measure – also paint a more
mixed picture. Longer-term expectations measures derived from financial markets and economists’
forecasts remain well anchored on the 2–3 per cent inflation target here in Australia (Graph 13, lower
panel). Other developed countries show broadly the same. But levels aren’t everything. Overseas

policy, as in other advanced economies.

The third reason for not taking too much comfort is the VUCA world is asking more of us. Volatility
means more inflation misses. Uncertainty means bigger expected forecast errors. Complexity
means more structural and cyclical drivers of inflation and employment to understand. Ambiguity
means genuinely unpredictable geopolitical policy actions. And weaker and more volatile supply
makes everything harder still – driving inflation up while activity and employment growth fall.
A final caution is that trust may be hard won but easily lost. It’s possible that our survey result
reflects reserves built up in earlier periods. Those reserves may protect trust against poor economic
outcomes for a time. But that may run out: there may be a breaking point we can’t see until we
reach it. Our survey can’t identify that threshold – but it isn’t one we want to test. A reserve of trust
is not a licence to disappoint. It makes the continuing work of earning trust more pressing, not less.
What is the RBA doing to keep strengthening public trust?
So how are we responding?

Start with values: ‘are they on my side?’ This was a point dear to the heart of Leslie Melville, who
helped shape the intellectual foundations for clause 8 of the
Commonwealth Bank Act 1945, which
charged the Bank with promoting ‘the economic prosperity and welfare of the people of Australia’,
and set out the dual mandate of price (originally currency) stability and full employment. That
mission remains fundamentally unchanged: it shapes everything we do.
But delivering on it is never easy. Gains tomorrow may require pain today – but those gains can be
hard to quantify, even after the event. And there can be winners and losers in the short run, even if
everyone is better off in the long run. Against that backdrop, the resilience in the trust scores
shown in Graph 10 is cautiously encouraging. But values-based trust is slow to build and quick to fall.
So we must continue to listen, through liaison, public engagement and the Community Research
Survey itself; learn how our decisions affect different people, especially the vulnerable; and level with
people: being open, honest, direct and evidence-based.
But good values aren’t enough: public trust also requires competence – and the persistence of
above-target inflation, and the growing threats from a VUCA world, mean central banks have work to
do to demonstrate we’re serious about achieving our policy goals. That’s why we’re investing heavily
in the skills and tools to navigate uncertainty. Four areas stand out:
First, understanding future economic challenges: our published research priorities
describe our key areas of enquiry (including AI, which our chief economist will speak about in
coming days). To keep our analysis rigorous and at the frontier, we’re launching a new external
engagement program with the research community, publishing more staff work via Insight
Notes, and establishing a financial markets research and modelling hub.
Second, investing in our models and frameworks: we’re extending our modelling suite to
explore and communicate alternative economic scenarios and their policy implications. The suite
includes: MARTIN, our core macroeconometric model; DINGO, a dynamic stochastic general
equilibrium model; HALLIE, a next-generation Heterogeneous Agent New Keynesian model; and
non-linear structural labour market models. In financial markets, we have been rolling out a
new monetary implementation framework; and our recently published
Framework for Additional
Monetary Policy Tools explains how we would use our balance sheet if rates again reached the
effective lower bound.
Third, better data and intelligence – including WHAM, a new Wealth and Housing Assets
Module developed with other government agencies and the Australian Bureau of Statistics;
partnerships with SEEK on labour market data, Macquarie University on BOSS and the Melbourne

Institute on household inflation expectations. Our liaison network holds around 900 meetings
annually with organisations across Australia: its enormous qualitative and quantitative database
can be analysed with increasingly sophisticated AI tools. And our Community Research Survey
allows us to explore key economic issues for Australian households.
Fourth, better decisions under uncertainty: we increasingly test alternative scenarios
alongside the central forecast. How could the outlook differ – and how should policy respond?
We also regularly review our past forecasts and judgements to learn from them. The question
isn’t simply ‘what do we think will happen?’; it’s also ‘what if things turn out differently?’
But even with better analysis and policy, a VUCA world brings more unexpected inflation misses and
uglier trade-offs. And that risks eroding trust in our capacity and commitment, unless we continue to
improve our ability to explain ourselves clearly, specifically:
Why inflation is away from target: unexpected shocks, deliberate tradeoffs, limits in our
forecasts and analysis, or the timing and calibration of policy? People need that account to judge
whether we’re acting in the public interest, with integrity and competence.
How we expect the economy to evolve: including the risks and uncertainties, drawing on our
analysis of the underlying shocks and the economic mechanisms involved.
Our strategy for returning inflation to target: how we balance full employment against the
speed of disinflation; and how we might respond to material risks that have not yet crystallised.
Not a promised rate path – ‘forward guidance’ – but a reaction function that gives people
confidence we’ll achieve our goals. That’s an important distinction: explaining how we would
respond as circumstances change is different from promising what interest rates will be. We need
people to understand the strategy, not mistake it for a guarantee.
Research into how our communications affect different people’s expectations helps guide this work:
Financial market expectations are important for influencing short-term interest rates and other
financial prices, transmitting policy to the economy. Recent research has found that talking about
issues like housing risks and wages can shape the weight markets think we’ll place on bringing
inflation back to target in a timely way. But sometimes actions speak louder than words.
Communicating with the public requires different tools. Households interpret economic news
and its interest rate implications very differently from markets (Graph 15). Our survey finds
that people pay attention to our actions and communications, and want to know more. But that

understanding has important gaps, including on how higher interest rates affect inflation – perhaps
monetary policy’s most important single relationship.
So we’re investing in new more targeted communications for households and businesses. The
Governor’s media conference is focused on explaining the Board’s decisions, their drivers and what it
could mean for people. A more layered approach combines detailed publications like the
Statement
on Monetary Policy with shorter summaries, website explainers and digital content for different levels
of interest and economic knowledge. Recent research finds that even simple explainers can
significantly improve accessibility and understanding of key economic relationships, and increase
trust. Experience so far is positive, including better survey scores for openness, honesty and
comprehensible explanations. But we must continue to refine our approach: the aim is not to make
everyone read the longest publication, it’s to help people find an explanation they can use, without
losing the substance of what we’re trying to say.

Accountability brings these elements together. We answer ultimately to the public, including through
parliamentary appearances and other public engagement. New five-year reviews of the RBA will
strengthen that accountability further, providing periodic assessments of performance and
opportunities for improvements. Being independent must never mean being beyond challenge.
These elements must all work together. Clear communication means little unless the actions being
taken are pushing us towards good outcomes. Competent action isn’t enough if people don’t
understand the need for difficult economic outcomes or trust that we’re trying to do what’s best for
the economy. And blind faith in our values cannot by itself sustain trust in a technocratic institution.
None of these is a substitute for the others. We need the values, the competence, the explanation
and the scrutiny. That’s what makes earning trust a continuing task.
Conclusion
Let me conclude. For a central bank, trust is not a nice-to-have. It’s integral to how monetary policy
works. When people trust us to deliver the goals we’ve been asked to pursue, policy is more effective
and less costly.
To earn that trust, people must have confidence that we’re on their side, up to the job, and capable
of explaining ourselves. Trust in the RBA seems to have held up through a difficult period. But the
warnings are clear: some people’s confidence in delivery, particularly on inflation, has fallen. And a
VUCA world will keep testing us.
Let me end where I began, with Leslie Melville. In 1931, the Great Depression drove deep fissures
into public confidence, and demanded brave and innovative thinking. In response, Melville built what
has become the RBA’s Economic Group: establishing the data, analytical frameworks and judgement
needed to contribute to the major debates of post-war reconstruction. That combination of public
purpose, expertise and evidence helped the Bank earn trust in those difficult years.
There is an irony in the fact that the Bank of England that recommended Melville was led by
Montague Norman, whose maxim was ‘never explain, never excuse’. Thankfully, those days are long
behind us. Trust is no birthright. We never stop earning it – by continuing to listen, learn and level
with the people we serve. To invest in the skills and tools to deliver. Explain our decisions. And
welcome scrutiny, including when outcomes fall short.
Melville would have expected nothing less.

In the sociological literature, the first meaning is sometimes called ‘granted trust’ and the second ‘earned trust’.11[]
As Onora O’Neill points out in her Reith lectures, transparency and accountability can never fully close the expertise
gap, because no matter how many layers of audit and independent assessment are wrapped around an expert
endeavour, the non-expert must always rely on some expert’s judgement – whether it be the original actor, or
additional experts brought in to assess their reasoning and work. At some point in this potential infinite regress,
there must be an act of faith. O’Neill argues that where people choose to draw this line can be better assessed by
observing what they do rather than what they say. She notes that people frequently place journalists towards the
lower end of trust rankings – yet they devour even poorly sourced press critiques, or gossip, relating to high-profile
public figures. Another common tension lies in the contrast between statements that people have little trust in
institutions and companies and the trust given to technology providers to protect the types of private data that
previous generations would never have dreamed of disclosing. Whether actions or words end up predominating, the
bottom line is that a trust-less life is impossible – and technology, far from obviating the need for trust, frequently
demands it more than ever.
