Economic Activity, Prices,
and Monetary Policy in Japan
Speech at a Meeting with Local Leaders in Nagasaki
1
I. Current Situation and Outlook for Economic Activity and Prices
Economic Activity
The Bank of Japan assesses the current situation of Japan's economy as follows: "Japan's
economy has recovered moderately, although some weakness has been seen in part, partly
due to the impact of the situation in the Middle East." To elaborate on this point, I would like
to examine several elements of the economy, starting with developments in overseas
economies.
Regarding overseas economies, uncertainties heightened considerably in spring 2026,
reflecting the situation in the Middle East, but since then, real economic activity has not seen
a significant downward deviation on the whole (left panel of Chart 1). Against this backdrop,
the Bank assesses that "overseas economies have grown moderately on the whole, although
some weakness has been seen in part." Looking at international commodity prices, crude oil
prices have been at fairly high levels, as the situation in the Middle East remains unclear, but
they have fallen below their peak levels reached in April (right panel of Chart 1). On the other
hand, copper prices have remained elevated, partly reflecting strong global AI-related
demand.
Examining the trends in external demand for Japan's economy amid these overseas
developments, the effective exchange rate of the yen has been on a depreciating trend recently
(left panel of Chart 2). In this situation, exports have increased moderately (right panel of
Chart 2). By type of goods, increased exports of IT-related goods and capital goods, reflecting
solid global AI-related demand, have been the driving force of overall exports.
Corporate profits have been at high levels, supported by (1) the solid increase in global AI-
related demand, (2) resilience in domestic demand, and (3) progress in the pass-through of
cost increases to selling prices reflecting this resilience (left panel of Chart 3). Business
sentiment has also been at a more favorable level (right panel of Chart 3). Reflecting solid
corporate profits, business fixed investment has been on a moderate increasing trend, despite
being affected by a rise in construction costs (left panel of Chart 4). Moreover, business fixed
investment plans in the Tankan (Short-Term Economic Survey of Enterprises in Japan) have
shown an overall solid increase in investment (right panel of Chart 4).
2
Moving on to consumption, the employment and income situation, which is important when
assessing private consumption, has improved moderately. Labor market conditions have
remained tight, as indicated by the unemployment rate being at a low level of around 2.5
percent (left panel of Chart 5). Under these circumstances, wages have continued to increase
steadily, albeit with fluctuations (right panel of Chart 5). The year-on-year rate of increase in
scheduled cash earnings has been at around 3 percent, as the wage growth rate in terms of the
rate of base pay increases for 2026 turned out to be around the same level as in the previous
year, as a result of the annual spring labor-management wage negotiations. Special cash
earnings (bonuses etc.) have maintained their firm growth, as seen in summer bonuses for
2026 increasing from the previous year, supported by the high levels of corporate profits. In
addition, the minimum wage had its second-largest increase on record this year. Moreover,
the year-on-year rate of change in real wages has been positive since the turn of 2026,
supported by the growth in nominal wages.
Private consumption has been resilient against the background of the improvement in the
employment and income situation, despite the impact of factors such as price increases (Chart
6). Given recent data, however, I personally believe consumption has not necessarily been
strong. The breakdown of real GDP for the April-June quarter of 2026 shows that private
consumption has remained almost flat, although I understand that this is due to a number of
institutional factors. Developments in the Consumption Activity Index (CAI) also suggest
that it is appropriate to consider private consumption as being more or less flat. The Bank
assesses private consumption as being resilient, and in this sense, I would not go so far as to
say private consumption has been weak; however, I believe it is currently not so strong that
it will be the driving force of Japan's economic growth as a whole.
Prices
Next, I would like to explain price developments. With the aforementioned rise in commodity
prices since the turn of 2026, the year-on-year rate of change in Japan's import price index on
a yen basis has been significantly positive, due to a rise in the rate of increase in the index on
a contract currency basis -- reflecting the situation in the Middle East and global AI-related
demand -- as well as to the impact of the yen's depreciation (left panel of Chart 7). The year-
on-year rate of increase in the producer price index (PPI), which is based on corporate
3
transaction prices, has marked a high rate of around 7 percent, reflecting price increases in
the upstream of the supply chain (right panel of Chart 7). The key issue is how fast and to
what extent price rises in the upstream and midstream exert upward pressure on consumer
prices in the downstream.
Let us look at developments in the consumer price index (CPI). Despite the effects of the
government's measures to reduce the household burden of higher energy prices, the year-on-
year rate of increase in the CPI for all items excluding fresh food has been at around 2 percent
recently, as the effects of the rise in import prices have been emerging, with moves to pass
on wage increases to selling prices continuing (left panel of Chart 8). The rate of increase in
the CPI for all items excluding fresh food and energy has been at around 2.5 percent recently,
despite slower growth in food prices that surged in the previous year. As another CPI figure
published by the Bank, the rate of increase in the CPI for all items excluding fresh food and
institutional factors -- such as the government's measures to reduce the household burden of
higher energy prices and the provision of free education -- has been in the range of 3.0-3.5
percent (right panel of Chart 8).
Recent estimates of inflation levels differ noticeably depending on whether institutional
factors are excluded or not. In terms of the relevance to real economic activity, while I believe
it is important to examine price developments that consumers are actually facing, there is a
certain degree of rationality in excluding institutional factors to capture the trend in consumer
prices. Moreover, in considering the conduct of monetary policy, it is crucial to grasp
developments in underlying inflation, which I will explain later in the section on monetary
policy conduct.
Outlook and Risks
I would now like to discuss the outlook and risks for economic activity and prices.
Regarding the outlook for economic activity, economic growth is likely to slow somewhat in
the near term in Japan, due to the rise in energy and raw material prices since early spring
reflecting the impact of the situation in the Middle East. However, Japan's economy is
expected to continue growing moderately, albeit at a decelerated rate, as the economy is likely
4
to be underpinned by, for example, the government's various measures -- such as those to
reduce the household burden of higher energy prices -- and accommodative financial
conditions, in addition to the increase in global AI-related demand. From 2027 onward, the
economic growth rate is likely to rise moderately, since it is projected that the adverse effects
of high energy and raw material prices will wane and that a virtuous cycle from income to
spending will gradually intensify.
As for the outlook for prices, the year-on-year rate of increase in the CPI is likely to accelerate
to a level clearly above 2 percent from the second half of fiscal 2026, mainly affected by the
rise in energy prices to date, the increase in global AI-related demand, and the recent
depreciation of the yen. Thereafter, with the waning of the effects of high crude oil prices,
the rate of increase is expected to decline toward around 2 percent in the second half of the
projection period of the October 2026 Outlook for Economic Activity and Prices (Outlook
Report).
There are three major risk factors regarding the outlook for economic activity and prices: the
impact of the situation in the Middle East, developments in AI-related demand, and the effects
of developments in foreign exchange rates. Although the direction of each risk varies, the
overall risk balance assessed in the October Outlook Report is that "risks to economic activity
are generally balanced," while risks to the outlook for the CPI "are skewed to the upside."
Offering my own perspective on these risks, I believe it is necessary to pay attention to the
possible effects of past policy interest rate hikes on real economic activity. While there are
indeed several upside risks to prices, I consider that the extent to which the risk factors could
actually push up prices is uncertain, and that it is also necessary to closely monitor the effects
of policy rate hikes on prices through any downturns in real economic activity. I will address
these points further in the next section on monetary policy conduct.
5
II. Conduct of Monetary Policy
Financial Conditions and Underlying Inflation
The Bank decided to raise the policy interest rate at its Monetary Policy Meetings (MPMs)
held in June and September 2026, bringing the rate to 1.25 percent for the first time in around
three decades (left panel of Chart 9).
In examining the effects of policy interest rate hikes on real economic activity, nominal
interest rates show that bank lending rates and issuance yields for CP and corporate bonds
have increased due to a rise in market interest rates, implying that the degree of
accommodation in financial conditions has been reduced (right panel of Chart 9). On the other
hand, real interest rates in the short-term zone -- which affect economic activity substantially
-- have been negative; financial conditions thus still seem to be accommodative (left panel of
Chart 10). Real interest rates in the medium- to long-term zone appear to have entered positive
territory, but even under these circumstances, the diffusion index (DI) for firms' financial
positions in the Tankan suggests that these positions have shown no deterioration so far and
have remained at favorable levels (right panel of Chart 10). Nevertheless, together with the
cumulative effects of policy interest rate hikes implemented to date, I believe it is necessary
to closely monitor the effects of rate hikes on real economic activity, such as firms' financing
conditions and household loans, since in my view it is likely to take one year or more for the
impact of a rate hike to become clear.
Another important factor when considering monetary policy is the trend in prices. In this
regard, medium- to long-term inflation expectations are at around 2 percent, and underlying
CPI inflation is at a level that is generally consistent with the price stability target of 2 percent
(Chart 11). The October 2026 Outlook Report points out the following: "When the Bank was
in the process of raising underlying CPI inflation toward 2 percent, downside risks to prices
warranted considerable attention. However, there has recently been a shift in the phase of
monetary policy, and looking ahead, it is necessary to pay due attention to keep the risk of
underlying CPI inflation deviating upward to a level above the price stability target of 2
percent from materializing and thereby exerting an adverse impact on the economy
afterward" (Chart 12).
6
In light of these developments, the Outlook Report states that it is appropriate to continue to
raise the policy interest rate and adjust the degree of monetary accommodation, in response
to developments in economic activity and prices as well as financial conditions. With these
points in mind, I would now like to present my views on monetary policy.
My Views on the Conduct of Monetary Policy
At the June and September MPMs mentioned earlier, I voted against raising the policy interest
rate. The reason is that, as noted in the statements released after the meetings, with the
inflation rate being below 2 percent recently, I considered that it could not necessarily be said
that the economic situation was strong, and it was therefore desirable for the Bank to maintain
the policy rate level. At the July and October MPMs, I voted for keeping the policy rate
unchanged.
Let me explain the background to my position. According to standard macroeconomic theory,
the rationale for raising the policy interest rate to address actual price rises or the risk of rising
prices is that higher interest rates exert downward pressure on consumption and investment,
thereby reducing upward pressure on prices. In light of this, I believe that a key issue for the
decision whether to raise the policy interest rate depends on which should be given greater
weight: the impact on the economy of inflation expected in the future, or the downside impact
on the economy of the rate hike.
Regarding the impact of policy rate hikes on the economy, downside risks may not be
particularly large when financial conditions are extremely accommodative, since they will
likely remain accommodative even after a policy rate hike. On the other hand, it should be
noted that downside risks to the economy posed by a policy rate hike will gradually increase
if the continued rate hikes have tightened financial conditions or have reduced the degree of
accommodation.
The neutral interest rate is an indicator used to assess whether the current level of interest
rates is accommodative or not. The Bank estimates that the natural rate of interest is currently
around slightly above minus 1 percent to plus 0.5 percent. The neutral interest rate -- which
is calculated by adding the expected rate of inflation to the natural rate of interest -- is
7
therefore estimated to be around slightly above 1 percent to 2.5 percent. While the estimated
range is quite broad, the policy interest rate is already within this range. Given this, although
financial conditions have been accommodative, the degree of accommodation seems to be
gradually diminishing. In other words, policy interest rate hikes are now becoming more
likely to have a larger impact on real economic activity than when the Bank began to raise
the policy rate, which was clearly below the neutral interest rate. Considering in particular
that, as I mentioned earlier, real economic activity -- especially private consumption -- has
not necessarily been strong recently, I believe due attention should be paid to the risk that
policy rate hikes will slow the economy and thereby push down underlying inflation.
Turning to the upside risks to prices, I believe that, as stated in the October 2026 Outlook
Report, it is necessary to be attentive to the risk of adverse effects on Japan's economy if
underlying inflation rises above 2 percent. On the other hand, I think that consumer prices,
and ultimately underlying inflation, are subject to downside risks if the past and any future
policy interest rate hikes lead to a deterioration in real economic activity. As the year-on-year
rate of increase in consumer prices that households are actually facing has accelerated
gradually but, partly due to the effects of government policies, is currently at around 2 percent,
I consider it better to carefully assess developments in actual consumer prices before judging
how far prices may rise and how likely it is that underlying inflation will in turn rise beyond
2 percent.
Based on these points, if the Bank is to raise the policy interest rate further, it will likely be
under circumstances where higher wages spur demand-driven inflation and the mechanism
in which wages and prices rise moderately in interaction with each other is thereby operating
to a greater degree. In this regard, I consider it desirable that the year-on-year rate of change
in real wages has remained positive since the turn of 2026, supported by substantial base pay
increases for three consecutive years and by rising bonuses. However, with economic activity
-- particularly private consumption -- not showing strong momentum for growth, I believe
that the recent price increases have not necessarily been driven by demand factors. In this
sense, I consider it necessary for the Bank to give due consideration to the pace of any policy
rate hikes.
8
Change in the Pace of Sales of Exchange-Traded Funds
In closing, I would like to talk about the Bank's sales of exchange-traded funds (ETFs).
At the September 2025 MPM, the Bank decided to begin selling its holdings of ETFs, and
has proceeded with the sales since January 2026. So far, it has been conducting these sales
smoothly while avoiding a major impact on the market, and the trading value in the stock
market has been expanding. Given these developments, the Bank decided at the October 2026
MPM to change the annual pace of sales, increasing the scale from about 0.05 percent to
about 0.1 percent of the trading value in the market.
As a result, the pace of sales will increase from 330 billion yen per year to 860 billion yen
per year, and the time required to complete the sales will shorten from 112 years to about 43
years. Nevertheless, the framework will remain the same in that a temporary adjustment in
the sales amount or a suspension of sales may be made within the range of the annual pace of
sales in response to changes in financial market conditions.
Thank you.
