SPEECH
The science and practice of monetary policy
communication: the ECB experience
Keynote speech by Philip R. Lane, Member of the Executive Board
of the ECB, at the Bank of England and its Watchers Conference
London, 10 November 2026
Monetary policy is about more than just the latest interest rate decision. It also involves communication
about future economic developments and the factors shaping future policy decisions.[ ] By steering the
expectations of financial market participants, central bank communication shapes the full set of
financial market prices across the maturity and risk spectrums, in turn affecting financing conditions and
decisions around credit, saving and investment. The decisions of households and firms are also
affected by their beliefs about monetary policy, which depend on how the central bank communicates.
[ ]
I will divide this speech into two parts. In the first part, I will review recent contributions by ECB staff on
the science of monetary policy communication.[ ] In the second part, I will discuss the practice of
monetary policy communication from an ECB perspective.
The science of monetary policy communication
Greater data availability, increased computing power and artificial intelligence have substantially
expanded the tools available for analysing central bank communication. Until recently, researchers
relied on dictionary-based approaches to quantify the content and tone of monetary policy statements
and speeches. This involved categorising specific words and expressions associated with a restrictive
or accommodative stance, or with different assessments of inflation and the economic outlook.[ ]
Machine learning techniques and large language models (LLMs) now allow for the extensive analysis of
large volumes of text in a more automated manner, taking into account the context in which words are
used instead of relying solely on the frequency of individual words or predefined expressions.[ ]
Recent ECB research illustrates these possibilities. A set of novel, LLM-based in-house measures of
the tone of ECB monetary policy communication confirms that it becomes more hawkish during
tightening phases and more dovish during easing periods. These shifts in communication closely track
developments in the deposit facility rate (Chart 1, panel a).[ ] The importance of communication is also
illustrated by the research finding that information extracted from ECB monetary policy statements can
improve forecasts of euro area core inflation several quarters ahead, even when controlling for the
contributions of Eurosystem and ECB staff projections.[ ]
These indicators can also be employed to test whether central bank communication follows systematic
patterns that reflect the economic outlook and the policy stance. Indeed, the evidence suggests that

Financial markets
Financial market participants closely follow central bank communication because it provides information
about both the economic and inflation outlook and the expected path of monetary policy. Both of these
dimensions are relevant for asset pricing and investment decisions.

independently from actual policy changes.[ ] Research shows that financial analysts reassess the

From press conferences to
speeches: the impact of the ECB’s monetary policy communication
The importance of communication extends beyond Governing Council meetings. Research examining
thousands of speeches, interviews, hearings and other events shows that communication in the time
period between meetings can affect market interest rates across maturities. It can help markets
anticipate policy changes, particularly ahead of tightening episodes, and its cumulative effect over time
can be comparable to that of policy announcements, especially at longer maturities (Chart 2).[ ]
Beyond shaping expectations about the future path of policy rates, communication also affects financial
conditions more broadly. The tone of ECB communication can influence monetary policy uncertainty
and premia on risky assets.[ ] In particular, financial markets respond to several dimensions of
monetary policy news: the current policy rate, the expected path of rates, and market and sovereign
risk (“target”, “path”, and “risk” in Chart 3, respectively).

markets. Unlike professional market participants, most households do not closely follow central bank
communication. And even when they do encounter central bank messages, they may interpret their
implications differently to market analysts. Research presented in our 2021 monetary policy strategy
review suggests that information about the new inflation target reached only around one in ten

Reaching households, however, is just one part of the process. Attention to inflation varies with the
economic environment. When inflation is high, households and firms tend to pay more attention, while
they pay less attention when inflation is low and stable (Chart 5).[ ] This matters for the transmission of
monetary policy. What households and firms believe about future inflation, and how confident they are
that the central bank will deliver price stability, shapes how they set wages and prices and how they
adjust their spending and saving when we change interest rates.[ ]
When inflation is high, households tend to be more attentive, but they may also be less responsive to
information they already know. This underscores the importance of tailoring communication to the
economic environment to ensure that messages reach households when inflation is low while providing
clear, relevant and informative explanations when inflation becomes more salient.[ ]

Households typically interpret monetary policy in terms of how it directly affects their economic
circumstances. Euro area consumers tend to associate monetary policy primarily with borrowing costs
rather than directly with inflation. Those who emphasise the borrowing-cost channel may also respond
more strongly to a rate rise in their planned spending decisions.[ ]
The expectations of firms are also important for monetary policy transmission. Their views about future
inflation influence pricing, wage-setting and investment decisions, and ultimately the evolution of
inflation itself. Recent evidence shows that attention paid by firms to inflation varies considerably over
time, increasing when inflationary pressures become more pronounced (Chart 5, panel b).

nevertheless be significant (Chart 6).[ ]

information. Markets respond not only to the decision, but also to what it reveals about the assessment
of the economy. A surprise rate rise may signal tighter policy, but it may also lead markets to reassess
the strength of activity or the persistence of inflation. Research has developed methods to separate
policy surprises from the so-called central bank information effects, and more recently from surprises
about how the central bank responds to publicly available news. These distinctions are important in

For the ECB, research finds that estimated monetary policy surprises on meeting days – the
movements in interest rates in a narrow window around the announcement – are partially inferable from
euro area equity market changes over the six weeks preceding the meeting.[ ] Market reactions to
speeches, by contrast, show no such pattern.[ ] Information conveyed by ECB announcements also
travels across borders. The evidence suggests that it accounts for an important part of the co-
movement of German and US government bond yields around ECB announcement days.[ ]
ECB staff have also analysed the implications of uncertainty for monetary policy communication. When
economic conditions are changing rapidly, it may be preferable to place greater emphasis on the
current state of the economy than on precise indications about the distant future.[ ] The appropriate
degree of precision also depends on how confident policymakers are in their forecasts and how that
confidence is perceived.[ ] Moreover, how policymakers express confidence in their assessment – or
acknowledge developments that differ from expectations – can itself be informative about the policy
stance.[ ]
Trust and credibility
Finally, economic agents need to not only clearly understand central bank messages but also trust that
the words will be followed by action. Explaining why the ECB’s inflation target matters for economic
stability can strengthen public confidence in our commitment to keep prices stable. Such explanations
are particularly effective among people with lower financial literacy, as they can help them understand
how monetary policy contributes to achieving the inflation target. The effects can persist for several
months but tend to fade over time.[ ] Building and maintaining credibility therefore requires more than
communicating individual decisions clearly. It requires explaining, consistently and repeatedly, why our
objective matters and how our policies contribute to achieving it.[ ]
The practice of monetary policy communication
Having reviewed recent scientific research on monetary policy communication in the first part of this
speech, I will now turn to a discussion of monetary policy communication in practice.
Throughout its history, the ECB has adapted its communication content to changes in the economy, the
instruments activated in setting the monetary policy stance and the nature of the uncertainty facing
policymakers (Chart 7).

Before the global financial crisis, ECB monetary policy communication focused on explaining decisions
and making the underlying reaction function more predictable, without explicitly signalling an interest
rate path.[ ] Since its inception, the ECB has held press conferences to accompany its policy
decisions. The publication of staff macroeconomic projections from 2004 onwards provided further
information about the outlook. Policy decisions were systematically related to the assessment of risks
surrounding the projected inflation path, while communication between meetings could help markets
anticipate turning points. For instance, the speech by ECB President Trichet on 18 November 2005
ahead of the December 2005 interest rate increase helped prepare markets for the first interest rate
increase in five years.[ ] Through these steps, news about the orientation of monetary policy
influenced medium- and longer-term yields even before the era of explicit forward guidance.[ ]
The global financial crisis and the euro area sovereign debt crisis fundamentally changed the
environment. Amid severe financial stress, effective communication played an essential role in restoring
confidence and supporting smooth monetary policy transmission. This was particularly evident in the

As policy rates approached the effective lower bound, influencing expectations about future rates
became all the more important. The ECB introduced explicit rate forward guidance in July 2013. By
communicating on the future policy path, rate forward guidance sought to influence market
expectations and financing conditions beyond the immediate policy horizon. Experience showed that
design matters. Longer-horizon and state-contingent guidance can reduce uncertainty about future
rates by clarifying the conditions under which policy may change.
By contrast, short-horizon or open-ended guidance may be less effective and can even increase
uncertainty if it leaves markets unsure about the central bank's future course of action.[ ] The
interaction with other instruments matters as well. Asset purchases can reinforce the effectiveness of
forward guidance, while clear information about the scale of purchase programmes can reduce
uncertainty. However, linking the sequencing of asset purchases to interest rate decisions can also
constrain policy flexibility when economic conditions change unexpectedly.
As a result of the 2021 monetary policy strategy review, the Governing Council adopted the symmetric
two per cent inflation target. At the following monetary policy meeting (July 2021), it revised its rate
forward guidance by linking the expected policy path more explicitly to the inflation outlook and realised
progress in underlying inflation. This reinforced the commitment to a forceful or persistent monetary
policy response aimed at achieving the inflation target when inflation appeared at risk of undershooting
the target.[ ] [ ]
However, in the wake of the 2021-2022 inflation surge, the guidance that had been designed
specifically for an environment of persistently below-target inflation was no longer appropriate. The
period between July 2022 and March 2023 constituted an important transition phase. In July 2022 the
Governing Council moved away from the effective lower bound by raising the policy rate from minus 50
basis points to zero.[ ] In line with the 2021 strategy decision that the ECB key interest rates are the
primary monetary policy instrument away from the effective lower bound, the monetary policy statement
did not provide specific guidance on the expected future rate path. However, it did provide directional
guidance, indicating that further rate normalisation would be appropriate at upcoming meetings, even if
the exact calibration would be data-dependent and decided on a meeting-by-meeting basis. The
following four meetings (September 2022 to February 2023) maintained directional guidance, with a
pair of 75 basis point hikes followed by a pair of 50 basis point hikes.
In addition, the February 2023 meeting articulated the intention to also hike by 50 basis points at the
March meeting, which was subsequently implemented. However, having quickly raised the policy rate
from -50 to 300 between July 2022 and March 2023, the March 2023 monetary policy statement
replaced directional guidance with the reaction function guidance that future interest rate decisions
would be based on the inflation outlook in light of incoming economic and financial data, underlying

inflation dynamics and the strength of monetary policy transmission. These criteria would be assessed
on a data-dependent, meeting-by-meeting basis. These elements of the reaction function remain in
place. An explicit role for the risks surrounding the inflation outlook was added in July 2025: this
reflected one implementation of the conclusion of the 2025 monetary policy strategy assessment that
monetary policy decisions should explicitly incorporate this risk assessment.
Although it turned out that the following four meetings would also deliver hikes (25 basis points at each
meeting, bringing the policy rate to 400 basis points), there was a clear gear shift at the March 2023
meeting. In essence, after a series of rapid and outsized hikes, the gap between the policy rate and the
plausible range of the “appropriate level” of the policy rate was no longer so wide as to justify ongoing
formal directional guidance.[ ] While formal directional guidance may be appropriate in the early
phases of a forceful monetary policy response to a large inflation shock in either direction, it may
convey an excessive level of certainty about the future rate path in the later phases (after a large
adjustment has already been put in place). Similarly, avoiding formal directional guidance is prudent in
implementing a measured monetary policy response to less extreme inflation shocks.[ ]
The framework guidance provided by the Governing Council can be interpreted as implementing the
strategic approach to making monetary policy decisions that was laid out in the 2021 monetary policy
strategy statement.[ ] In particular, the 2021 strategy committed to basing monetary policy decisions
on an integrated assessment of all relevant factors, taking into account the interdependencies between
the economic analysis of real and nominal variables and the monetary and financial analysis that
focused on the monetary transmission mechanism.
Since it would be unwise to rely exclusively on inflation projections, especially during times of high
uncertainty, a balanced approach that takes into account both the forward-looking inflation outlook and
the information revealed by the realised path of underlying inflation is preferable, with underlying
inflation indicators distilling the realised inflation data to uncover the components most relevant for the
medium term. In calibrating monetary policy, it is also essential to rely on the latest assessment of the
strength of monetary transmission, given the range of uncertainty about the timing and strength of the
macro-financial impact of shifts in the monetary policy stance and the scope for exogenous shocks to
financial conditions and financing conditions.[ ]
Framework guidance delivers a systematic approach to monetary policy that is interpretable by
financial market participants and external experts. The 2021 monetary policy strategy statement
highlighted that monetary policy decisions are communicated through the monetary policy statement,
the press conference, the Economic Bulletin and the monetary policy accounts. Together with the
publication of the quarterly staff macroeconomic projections exercises, these releases provide the
analytical underpinnings of how the reaction function criteria are assessed. Further analysis is provided
through staff working papers and contributions to the ECB Blog, along with presentations and speeches
by ECB Executive Board members.[ ]
Recent ECB research shows that the sensitivity of rate expectations to macroeconomic news appears
to have increased under the ECB’s current framework guidance regime compared with the period of
explicit forward guidance, where short-term rates were mostly constrained by an effective lower bound,
implying a smaller information content of surprises for the expected policy path (Chart 8). Markets

Finally, incorporating uncertainty into monetary policy communication poses trade-offs.[ ] In view of the
high level of external trust in the systematic and comprehensive analysis that underpin the exercise, the
information anchor provided by the baseline projections is more valuable than ever in an environment
of elevated uncertainty. At the same time, in relation to specific and material risk events, the publication
of alternative scenarios can help financial markets, experts and the public understand the possible
quantitative impact on inflation and growth dynamics if these alternative scenarios were to materialise.
[ ]
A communication risk is that excessive focus in placed on the published scenarios rather than also
taking into account the comprehensive risk assessment that is contained in each monetary policy
statement and expanded upon in the accounts. During any period, a range of risk factors can
materialise, such that a wide-angled perspective is needed, rather than taking a reductive approach
that overly focuses on any particular set of scenarios.[ ]
Conclusions
In this speech, I have outlined some recent ECB research on the science of monetary policy
communication. In particular, I have highlighted that new text-based tools, including large language
models, allow us to measure central bank communication more systematically, and these confirm that
what we say moves financial markets, between meetings as well as on meeting days. Relative to
markets-orientated communication, reaching households and firms is more challenging: their attention
to inflation rises and falls with inflation itself, which makes clear and consistent communication even
more important when inflation is elevated.
In relation to the practice of monetary policy communication, framework guidance is more effective than
rate forward guidance when policy rates are away from the effective lower bound. In particular,
framework guidance retains the beneficial properties of forward guidance in providing an anchor for
rate expectations without incurring the costs in terms of inflexibility and the risk of misperceptions. By
communicating a reaction function that spells out the criteria that guide our decisions, our framework
guidance has helped markets interpret the incoming data.

a frontier LLM (GPT/5.1) accompanied by an extensive prompt to support accuracy, auditability and
stability. The model version is kept fixed as performance across different vintages turns out very similar
for simple classification tasks and given that it facilitates comparing scores over time. Scores range
from -1 to 1 for each dimension: zero is neutral, positive is hawkish and negative is dovish. A hawkish
communication signals a higher probability of future tightening and/or prolonged restrictiveness across
the policy toolkit. A neutral communication emphasises data-dependence, balanced risks or no clear
directional tilt in the future policy stance. A dovish communication signals a higher probability of future
easing and/or earlier normalisation across the policy toolkit. The model also identifies the key passages
behind each score and provides a brief rationale for human validation. Each document is scored
multiple times, with the median retained to reduce sensitivity to individual model outcomes. It is worth
noting that the relation between the DFR and communication tone is inherently imperfect, as the latter
captures the language surrounding a policy decision that is measured independently from the decision
itself.
